Editorial Note

This work needs careful framing. Walther is not arguing here for race-based slavery or for the notion that some races were created by God to be enslaved. His basic claim in this article is different: he treats slavery as one of the burdens, inequalities, and civil relations of the fallen temporal order, and argues that the Gospel does not itself abolish such outward relations by political revolution.

For that reason, the article strongly opposes the humanistic and revolutionary ideals Walther associated with Rousseau, the French Revolution, and American founding figures such as Thomas Jefferson, Benjamin Franklin, and Thomas Paine. He is sharply critical of abolitionist agitation, and he treats abolitionist war-making spirits as bound up with broader revolutionary movements. At the same time, this page should not be read as a defense of cruelty. Walther also insisted that slaveholding abuses were sins to be judged by God, and this broader context includes his conviction that the South's devastation stood under divine punishment for the mistreatment of slaves.

This local note also makes explicit something historic Lutheran theology has long insisted on: Christians do not boast in race. Since Luther, confessional theology has taught that all men belong to one fallen human family, stand alike under sin, and need the same Christ. To boast in race is not to boast in righteousness, but in the sinful flesh from which all men alike need redemption.

Foreword

Walther opens by defining humanism as the belief that man can, by his own powers and by outward equality, possessions, and freedom, bring about a kind of heaven on earth. He describes it as the old temptation of the fallen world: instead of recognizing sin as the cause of misery and seeking help from God, man seeks his happiness in the creature and in earthly arrangements.

He traces that conflict through Scripture and church history. Cain's race seeks its salvation in the earth; Seth's line waits for the promised redemption. Paganism seeks the creature; the church lives as a pilgrim. In later centuries, Walther says, the same spirit reappears among radicals who dissolve property, hierarchy, and marriage in the name of earthly liberation. He explicitly connects this spirit to the peasant revolts, to Thomas Muenzer, to later revolutionary movements, and finally to the French Revolution's language of liberty, equality, and brotherhood.

That is the frame in which he turns to America. He argues that humanistic ideas exercised enormous influence in the nation's founding and public life, and he complains that American theology often praised revolutionary movements abroad, celebrated the republic as a model for the world, and allied itself with modern social crusades instead of testing everything by Scripture.

From there Walther narrows the issue: the question is not whether governments may choose emancipation for political reasons, but whether slavery in itself is to be condemned by Christians as inherently sinful. His answer is that abolitionism, when it treats every slaveholder as a criminal simply by reason of the relation itself, has been shaped not by the Gospel but by an anti-Christian humanistic program. Christians, he says, must finally ask not what seems plausible to modern feeling, but, "What is written?"

Scripture and Temporal Order

Walther next attempts to define slavery from Scripture and from older Lutheran usage. He treats it as a civil relation involving the loss of ownership, vocational freedom, and free movement, and argues that the biblical terms for servants in both Testaments do in many places refer to what modern readers would call slaves rather than hired workers.

He then gathers his biblical case. In the Old Testament, he notes Noah's curse, the households of Abraham and the patriarchs, the commandments that speak of servants as part of the household, and the Mosaic laws that regulate masters, slaves, debt service, release, injury, and purchase. From this he argues that slavery is not presented in Scripture as a creation ordinance like marriage, but as part of the burdens and penalties of a fallen world that nevertheless fall under God's moral regulation.

Walther presses the New Testament evidence even more strongly. Paul and Peter do not call slaves to revolt. Instead, they exhort masters to justice and fairness and exhort slaves to obedience, even when treated harshly, for the Lord's sake. For Walther, this means the apostles did not regard the master-slave relation as inherently unlawful, even though they sharply condemn kidnapping, cruelty, and every abuse of authority. He repeatedly insists that Christians must distinguish between the existence of an outward civil relation and the sins commonly committed within it.

One of his sharpest lines comes when he warns that the Gospel must not be turned into a political program of civil leveling. Christian freedom, he argues, is freedom before God from sin, death, and condemnation; it is not a divine command to erase every outward inequality in the present world. The Gospel, in his reading, does not abolish political order, property, authority, poverty, or servitude by a social revolution, but rather calls men in every station to repentance, faith, justice, and love.

Walther nevertheless does not excuse brutality. He points readers to biblical passages on the master's duties, to laws against man-stealing, and to the obligation of treating slaves with justice, fairness, and love. The true Christian task, in his view, is not to baptize a revolutionary ideology, but to restrain abuse and call those in authority to answer before God.

Old Lutheran Teachers

To show that his position is not novel, Walther assembles a string of older Lutheran authorities. Luther is central. In comments on 1 Corinthians 7, Luther distinguishes Christian freedom before God from outward civil freedom among men. The slave is the Lord's freedman, and the freedman is Christ's slave; therefore the Christian's standing before God does not erase the neighbor's outward claims in the temporal estate. Walther quotes Luther at length to make precisely that distinction: before God the conscience is free, but before the neighbor one must honor earthly obligations unless obedience to man requires disobedience to God.

He then turns to Melanchthon, Brenz, Cruciger, Chemnitz, Balduin, and Reichard. The common thread is the same. These writers reject the idea that the Gospel abolishes worldly order or civil law. They treat slavery as a severe burden of the fallen world, often as a punishment connected to sin, but not as something the Gospel cancels by direct political command. At the same time, they stress that masters remain under the fourth commandment's pattern of authority and must treat those under them with justice and humanity.

Several of the old writers also sharpen the distinction between spiritual and civil equality. In Christ, there is neither bond nor free with respect to salvation, mercy, or inheritance. But this spiritual equality does not itself collapse all outward stations. Walther uses these citations to argue that the old Lutheran tradition consistently refused to make Christian brotherhood the basis for political rebellion.

One especially revealing citation is Balduin's discussion of "brothers." Walther preserves Balduin's reminder that Scripture uses the term in more than one way and that, in the Christian sense, believers are brothers by affection and faith. That observation fits the broader Lutheran instinct this page highlights: the church does not ground its doctrine in racial boasting or blood-pride, but in one Creator, one human family under the fall, and one Redeemer for all who believe.

A Later Lutheran Theologian

Walther closes with a later Lutheran witness, G. C. A. von Harless. Harless argues that the Gospel does not produce its change by telling the slave to break his chains, but by turning both master and slave toward their higher Lord. The spirit of Christ does not sanctify cruelty or selfishness. It renews the content of the earthly relation by righteousness, mercy, and brotherly love.

Harless's formulation lets Walther end on a principle he wants to preserve throughout the article: "The Gospel does not abrogate external consequences and punishment for sin." It does not begin by changing every outward form. Instead, it penetrates the conscience and the heart. As truth takes hold, selfish misuse is judged, cruelty is rebuked, and men are taught to serve Christ within the stations in which they have been placed.

Walther includes this witness to reinforce his core anti-revolutionary claim. He wants readers to reject the language of abstract "human rights" when it is used as a doctrine allegedly derived from the Gospel itself. In his judgment, such language belonged first to heathen and humanistic moralizing, not to the apostolic doctrine. Christianity, he insists, addresses master and slave alike by giving both one Lord, one redemption, one mercy, and one final judgment.

The result is a work that is historically revealing, morally difficult, and unmistakably polemical. Read as a nineteenth-century confessional intervention, it shows Walther defending what he believed to be the difference between the Gospel and revolutionary politics, while also forcing modern readers to reckon with how that distinction was argued in the midst of America's slavery crisis.
